## Three layers of infrastructure

| Concept | What it is | You use it to |
|---|---|---|
| **Region** | A geographic area (e.g. `ap-southeast-1` Singapore), fully independent of other Regions | Be close to users; meet data-residency rules |
| **Availability Zone (AZ)** | One or more data centres with their own power, cooling and network. Every Region has **≥ 3 AZs**, kilometres apart | Tolerate failure: run in ≥ 2 AZs so one AZ going down doesn't take you down |
| **Edge location** | Hundreds of small sites worldwide | CloudFront (CDN) and Route 53 (DNS) serve users from the nearest one |

<Callout type="key">
**Golden rule:** anything important runs in **at least 2 AZs**. An AZ outage is rare but real, and your design must survive it.
</Callout>

## Choosing a Region

1. **Compliance** — must the data stay in a specific country? This rules Regions out first.
2. **Latency** — closer is faster. Users in Vietnam are typically served from Singapore (`ap-southeast-1`).
3. **Service availability** — new services usually launch in `us-east-1` first.
4. **Price** — the same service costs different amounts per Region. Singapore is often 10–30% more than N. Virginia.

## Who secures what? (Shared Responsibility)

- **AWS secures *the* cloud**: data centres, hardware, network, hypervisor.
- **You secure what's *in* the cloud**: your data, IAM, network configuration, encryption — and the operating system if you use EC2.

The more "managed" a service is (RDS, Lambda, DynamoDB), the less you do. You don't patch RDS's OS, but you still decide who can connect to it.
