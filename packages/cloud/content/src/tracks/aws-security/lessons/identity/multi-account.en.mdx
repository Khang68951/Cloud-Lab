As a company grows, putting everything in **one account** turns small mistakes into big incidents. The standard today is **many accounts** managed with **AWS Organizations**.

## Why many accounts?

| Reason | Why |
|---|---|
| **Blast radius** | Accounts are the strongest isolation boundary: a dev mistake cannot touch prod |
| **Billing** | Each account has its own bill — easy to see what each team spends |
| **Quotas** | Service limits are per account |
| **Simpler permissions** | "Who can enter prod" is easier than hundreds of tag-based policies |

## A suggested structure

```
Organization (management account — billing and governance only)
├─ OU Security:   Log Archive · Security Tooling
├─ OU Workloads:  app-prod · app-staging · app-dev
├─ OU Sandbox:    per-engineer experiment accounts (with budgets)
└─ OU Suspended:  accounts waiting to be closed
```

## The main tools

- **SCPs (Service Control Policies)** — guardrails for whole OUs: deny non-approved Regions, forbid disabling CloudTrail, forbid leaving the organisation.
- **IAM Identity Center** — people sign in **once**, pick an account and a *permission set*; all credentials are short-lived.
- **Cross-account roles** — CI/CD in a "tooling" account assumes a role in prod to deploy.
- **Control Tower** — sets up a best-practice landing zone (log accounts, guardrails, Account Factory).

<Callout type="key">
The **management account** is for governance and billing only. Run no workloads there — SCPs do not apply to it.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- IAM users for people in every account instead of Identity Center.
- CloudTrail logs stored in the workload account — an attacker who owns it can erase their tracks.
- Over-broad SCPs rolled out without testing on a small OU first.
